Why — the purpose

Macro Watch keeps an eye on the big picture so you don't have to: US stocks, interest rates, commodities, investor mood, the yen and Japan's inflation. It answers one question first — does anything need a look today? — and emails you when something crosses a level you chose or moves far more than usual.

It does not tell you to buy or sell anything. It is context for your own decisions.

What — the screens

  • Today: the answer first, then groups of indicators that belong together — US bonds, rates & the dollar; gold, silver & Dr. Copper; stocks & investor mood; the yen carry trade; credit, jobs & defaults ahead; oil; valuation. The most special group is at the top, and inside a group the most special indicator comes first. "Special" means a move far larger than usual over the last day, week or month, or a place at the edge of its own history — weighted by how much that indicator matters for stock investing. Every card says in plain words what it says. A group gets a short note under its title only when something in it really moves, explaining the move with the usual links between these markets (for example: gold falls when real yields and the dollar rise). That note is worked out from the numbers, not from news headlines. The small ? on a card turns into a red ! when there is a headline about it that you have not opened yet. Tap a card for a bigger chart; close it with the ✕ at its top right.
  • Overview: one map of everything that matters right now. The most important indicators are boxes, the links between them are lines, and the headlines about them hang on those boxes. It ranks on purpose and leaves the rest out. Things that usually move together and both are far out are joined by a white line; if one moves and its usual partner does not follow, the line is yellow and dashed. Tap a box to see how it is connected. The filters above the map narrow it to some groups, to linked boxes only, or to boxes that have a headline; point at a box to read why it is shown.
  • Heatmap: all companies of the S&P 500 or the Nasdaq-100 as one map — the size of a tile is the company's market value, the colour is today's change (green up, red down), grouped by sector. Point at a tile for details, tap it to open the quote. Data: Nasdaq's public screener and Wikipedia's index lists; if they are unreachable the app falls back to Yahoo prices and a built-in list of the biggest companies, and says so under the map.
  • Geopolitics: the headlines come first, ranked by importance and urgency (BREAKING, TODAY, EARLIER): talks and phone calls, statements by Merz, von der Leyen and others, new orders, company results of Leonardo, Saab, Rheinmetall and the like. Click a headline row for an AI summary, its title to open the article. Below: the biggest price moves, then the conflict as a map with the conflict in the middle and the markets around it. Russia–Ukraine: European and US defense stocks (rearmament), European gas, wheat. Middle East (Iran, Houthis, Saudi Arabia): oil, diesel, jet fuel, petrol, oil tankers, container shipping, airlines, the Saudi and Israeli markets, gold.
  • Market internals (a group on Today): how many of the 11 sectors rise (a falling index with most sectors up is a narrow sell-off in a few giants), the AI builders against the companies that use AI (banks, industrials), banks as a sign for the economy, breadth (equal-weight against size-weight), the volatility curve (VIX against the 3-month VIX) and the VVIX. Under it, big companies' reactions to earnings are set against the move the options market had priced in, where the data allows.
  • Extremes: indicators that only matter when they are far out on one side of their own history — the yen carry trade (USD/JPY, the US–Japan yield gap, Japan's yields), the Buffett indicator, Shiller P/E, credit spreads, the yield curve, the VIX and more. Each sits on a 0–100 scale (its percentile against all the history we have). The outer 5% on either side is an extreme (red), the outer 10% is stretched (amber); everything else is "normal range" and tucked away. Open "Yen carry trade in detail" at the bottom for USD/JPY, the interest gap and Japan's inflation with the Bank of Japan's forecasts.
  • Alerts: your levels (e.g. "USD/JPY above 160"), the email setup, the on/off switch for extreme alerts, and the history of every alert.

About extremes — please read

An extreme is not a prediction. Expensive markets can get more expensive for years, and a calm VIX can stay calm. An extreme says "this is unusual by historical standards, so look carefully" — it is context for your own decisions, not a buy or sell signal. The percentile only knows the history in the data (shown on each card as "since …"); some series are short, so read their percentile with care.

How — when it checks

  • Prices (stocks, VIX, commodities, dollar, yen): every 15 minutes, around the clock. Index values are the latest trade; futures trade almost 24 hours.
  • US interest rates and spreads (FRED): every 2 hours. These are official end-of-day figures, so they run one business day behind.
  • Monthly figures (inflation, Japan's 10-year yield and overnight rate): checked twice a day; you get an email when a new month's inflation figure appears.
  • News: headlines from free RSS feeds, every 15 minutes. Heatmap: every 15 minutes (Nasdaq screener, Wikipedia, Yahoo).
  • Prices, spending, jobs, growth and money supply: monthly or weekly figures from FRED (CPI, PCE, retail sales, unemployment, payrolls, job openings, GDP and others).
  • Extremes tab: long histories (decades) fetched every 3 hours from FRED, Yahoo Finance and multpl.com (Shiller P/E).
  • Bank of Japan forecasts: checked twice a day; the BoJ publishes new ones in January, April, July and October, and you get an email when it does.

AI summaries

Tap a headline card (not its title, which opens the article) for a short AI summary of the situation, built only from the headlines and the dashboard's current numbers: what the news says, what the numbers show, and how solid it is — whether the connected indicators confirm the story. It needs your own Anthropic API key, set on the Alerts tab. The AI is told not to add facts of its own, but it can still misread; check the sources before acting.

Motion

The button Motion in the top bar sets the speed of the animations: calm (default), slower, quick or off. The choice is remembered in this browser.

The ? on every card

Tap the small ? on a card to see how it is connected. The page switches to just that card, the indicators that explain it and the ones it moves, each with one sentence on how they usually behave and a tag that says whether they are behaving that way right now (as usual, or against the usual link, which means something else is driving it). Below that are the news headlines that fit. Tap a ? on any related card to follow the chain; ← Back or Esc returns.

News comes from free RSS feeds (CNBC, MarketWatch, Yahoo Finance, the Federal Reserve and Google News searches). Each headline is matched to indicators by keywords, so now and then a headline fits loosely. The app shows headlines and does not write its own explanation of them.

What the colours mean

  • Red: danger or a negative move — an extreme, credit stress, falling stocks, a rising fear gauge.
  • Yellow: a second, milder signal — "pay attention" (stretched, or unusually large move).
  • Green: positive — a good direction for investors (stocks up, fear down, defaults down) or an all-clear.
  • Blue: neutral accents — links, the selected tab, buttons, chart lines.
  • White and grey: plain information. Moves of things like interest rates, the dollar or gold stay white, because up or down is not clearly good or bad.

What an alert means

  • Level: a number crossed a level you set. You get one email when it crosses; it won't email again until the number has clearly gone back (by about 1%) and crosses again.
  • Unusual move: today's change is at least 2.5 times the typical daily change of the past year. On an ordinary day that happens for about 1 indicator in 80, so it is rare by design.
  • New figure: a new monthly inflation figure, or a new Bank of Japan forecast.
  • Extreme: an indicator on the Extremes tab entered the outer 5% of its history. One email on entering; it re-arms only after it is back in normal range (inside the outer 90%).

About the Fear & Greed number — please read

CNN's Fear & Greed Index cannot be read automatically from a server (CNN blocks it). So Macro Watch rebuilds it the way CNN describes it, from four of CNN's seven ingredients: stock momentum (S&P 500 against its 125-day average), volatility (VIX against its 50-day average), safe-haven demand (stocks' 20-day return minus Treasury bonds') and junk-bond demand (the junk-bond spread). Each is scored 0–100 by where it sits in its own last two years, and the four are averaged.

It follows CNN's direction but is not the same number — on the day it was built it read 22 while CNN showed 34 (both "fear"). The "Volatility vs normal" card is CNN's own volatility ingredient, calculated exactly as CNN describes it.

Where the numbers come from

  • Yahoo Finance — stock indexes, VIX, oil, gold, silver, copper, natural gas, dollar index, USD/JPY.
  • FRED (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis) — Treasury yields, yield curve, mortgage rate, junk-bond spread, Fed funds rate, US CPI, Japan 10-year yield and overnight rate.
  • multpl.com — the Shiller P/E table. FRED also supplies the Buffett indicator's inputs, financial conditions, the Sahm rule, consumer sentiment, long yen history, jobless claims, junk-bond spreads and the Federal Reserve's survey of bank lending standards (quarterly).
  • BIS (Bank for International Settlements) — Japan's official consumer price inflation.
  • Bank of Japan — the Policy Board's inflation forecasts from each quarterly Outlook Report.

If a source can't be reached, the app keeps the last good numbers and says so under the summary on the Today tab.
